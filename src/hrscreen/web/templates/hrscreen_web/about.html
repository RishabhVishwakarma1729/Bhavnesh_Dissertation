Method & limits

What happens to a resume, how the models differ, and what the results cannot tell you.

What happens to a resume

  1. Text is extracted from the PDF, DOCX or TXT file.
  2. Emails, phone numbers and web links are removed so they cannot influence the score.
  3. Skills (from a 380-skill list), years of experience and education level are read with simple rules.
  4. The model scores the resume against the job description; resumes are sorted by score.
  5. The ranking, not the resume text, is saved.

The models

  • Keyword rules: share of the job's skills the resume mentions. The ATS-style baseline.
  • TF-IDF: weights words that are distinctive to the job; cosine similarity.
  • Embeddings: a pre-trained language model (MiniLM) compares meaning, in chunks.
  • XGBoost: learns from labelled pairs; used in the benchmark, not in this screening form.

What the results cannot tell you

  • The benchmark's ground truth is a proxy. A resume is "relevant" when its dataset category matches the job. That measures agreement with labels, not with a recruiter, and related categories (a Python developer is a fine data-scientist candidate) count as misses.
  • Experience and education are guesses from text patterns and can be wrong or missing.
  • The fairness audit has no protected attributes. It infers education and experience groups from text and uses gender-coded names as a probe. It cannot show discrimination against real protected groups.
  • Rank-correlation numbers are not used to compare models because a rare yes/no label caps them and ties inflate them.
  • The job descriptions and skill list are small and editable. Results depend on them.
  • A shortlist is advice, not a decision. Keep a person responsible for every hiring decision.

Where to read more

The full method, formulas and evaluation protocol are in docs/METHODOLOGY.md in the repository.
